📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
A page can hold words and pictures at once. Can you build one where the words and the marks say a single thing together?
This is the final unit of the year. It pulls your whole toolkit back together. You will combine materials into mixed media, and you will use line, shape, value, contrast, and unity at once on a single page that is part poem and part picture. Word and image can be built together from scratch, the way you will see below, or a page can be transformed by covering most of an existing text, the way your own studio project will work.
👀 PART 1 · LOOK FIRST

Before we name anything, just look. Spend a full minute with the page below. Try to read the words in order, then notice everything happening around them.

William Blake wrote this poem and designed this picture together, then etched and hand-printed the whole page himself, coloring each copy by hand so no two are quite alike. He called this method illuminated printing. The words are not sitting on top of a separate illustration. They are part of the same design, lettered into the same dark, glowing surface as the tiger. Read the poem, then look at how the picture around it makes the words feel even louder.

💬 WONDER FIRST
Cover the tiger with your hand and just read the words. Then uncover it and look again. Does the picture change how the poem feels? Hold your answer. By the end of this lesson you will have the words for how a page like this is built, and for a very different way later artists learned to build one.
💭 QUICK CHECK-IN · NO WRONG ANSWER
After viewing the page, which response feels closest to yours?

Each of those answers points at the same idea: a page can be built so that its words and its picture are not two separate things, but one unified surface. Now learn to name the tools that do it.

📚 PART 2 · NAME WHAT YOU SEE

Mixed media means combining more than one material or technique in a single artwork. Blake's plate is mixed media: etched line, printed ink, and hand-applied watercolor all live on one surface, and the lettering itself is drawn, not typed. A page like this works only when the elements and principles you learned all year cooperate. Four of them carry most of the weight here.

LineThe bold outline of the tiger's body and the hand-lettered strokes of the poem, both drawn by the same hand.
ShapeThe tiger's striped body and the flame-like forms in the background. Organic, not geometric.
ValueHow light or dark each area is. The dark, inked ground pressing against the glowing tiger is the engine of the whole page.
ContrastA strong difference placed side by side. The bright tiger against the black field is pure contrast.

Contrast: how the words and the picture get noticed

Contrast is a strong, noticeable difference between two things placed near each other: light against dark, busy against empty, sharp against soft. Blake builds contrast with value, setting a bright figure against a dark ground so the eye cannot look away.

Almost two hundred years after Blake, some artists found a very different way to make word and image share a page. Instead of writing a new poem and drawing a new picture together, they began with a page someone else had already printed, painted over most of the words, and let a short new poem hide inside the few words left showing. This method is called blackout poetry, also called erasure poetry. It became well known through the artist Tom Phillips, who began treating the pages of an old Victorian novel this way in 1966 and kept reworking the book for over fifty years; that project, called A Humument, is now held at Tate, London. The method is simple to say and hard to do well: choose a few words that, read in order, make a phrase worth keeping, cover everything else, then illustrate the page so the picture and the surviving words say the same thing. Value contrast is still the engine, exactly as it is in Blake's plate. The dark covering goes dark, and the few kept words stay light, so the eye lands on them first. Imagine an old printed page where all but a handful of words are painted over in solid black: the few words left showing, read in order, become a short new poem, and the strong light-against-dark difference is what makes them easy to find. You will build exactly that page in this week's studio project.

Unity: making the page feel like one thing

Unity is the sense that all the parts of an artwork belong together as a single whole. Blake builds unity by repeating his fiery color and his bold outline across both the tiger and the lettering, so the poem and the picture read as one design rather than two things sharing a page. On a blackout page, unity is harder to earn, because you are joining someone else's printed text, a dark covering, and your own drawing. Artists build it the same way Blake did: by repeating a color, a shape, or a kind of mark across the whole page, and by letting the illustration grow out of the poem's meaning instead of sitting beside it. A page has weak unity when its colors and shapes are unrelated and scattered to the corners, so the eye cannot tie them together. It has strong unity when one repeated color and shape are threaded along a single path, so every part reads as one design. Aim for the second.

💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
A quick test for your finished blackout page: cover the kept words with your thumb. The picture alone should still feel like one page, with a clear path for the eye and a clear light-against-dark contrast. Then uncover the words. If the picture and the poem agree, you have unity, the same quality Blake built by hand almost two hundred years earlier.
🔍 PART 3 · WATCH IT WORK

Artists and critics look at art in a deliberate order, so that judgment is earned and not just blurted. The four steps spell DAIJ: Describe, Analyze, Interpret, Judge. Watch it applied to Blake's The Tyger. You will use the same steps in your VR critique this week, on a work of your own choosing.

Describe (just the facts)

A single hand-printed, hand-colored book page. A tiger drawn in bold dark outline stands against a glowing orange and black background. Hand-lettered verse is worked into the design above and below the figure, along with small drawn foliage.

Analyze (how is it built?)

The page is mixed media: etched line, printed ink, and hand-applied watercolor on one surface. Line appears in the tiger's outline and in the hand-drawn lettering. Value contrast sets the bright tiger against the dark ground so the eye finds it first. The repeated fiery color and bold line across both the picture and the words give the page unity, so the poem and the picture read as one object rather than two.

Interpret (what is it saying?)

Blake is showing that a poem does not have to sit apart from its picture; the two can be made at once, by one hand, so that neither fully makes sense without the other. The tiger's fierce, glowing presence argues that a page can hold real force when word and image are built as a single design.

Judge (does it succeed?)

It succeeds. The poem reads clearly, the tiger's presence matches its mood, and contrast and unity hold the whole page together as one thing. That same balance of found or kept words and a built picture, whether written new like Blake's or uncovered from an old page like your own project, is exactly what you will aim for.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND YOUR BLACKOUT PAGE
An AI tool can generate a poem from a prompt, or erase parts of an image automatically. It cannot make the choices that are the heart of this project: which found words on your page carry meaning, which words to cover, and which marks to draw so the picture and the poem agree. Those selections, and the work of covering and illustrating the page yourself, are what is being graded here.
📝 VOCABULARY
Mixed MediaAn artwork that combines more than one material or technique on a single surface.
Illuminated PrintingBlake's method of etching, printing, and hand-coloring text and image together on one plate.
Blackout PoetryA poem made by keeping a few words on an existing page and covering the rest. Also called erasure poetry.
Found TextExisting words, not written by the artist, used as raw material for new work.
ContrastA strong difference between two things placed near each other, such as light against dark.
UnityThe sense that all the parts of an artwork belong together as one whole.
LineA path made by a moving point; here, both the tiger's outline and the hand-lettered words.
ValueThe lightness or darkness of an area; the engine of contrast on both Blake's plate and a blackout page.
Public DomainWork whose copyright has expired, free for anyone to use and alter.
Derivative WorkA new work built on top of an existing one; needs permission unless the source is public domain or your own.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

This is where the whole year meets. Line leads the eye, value and contrast make the words findable, and unity ties the poem to the picture so the page reads as one thing. Blake built his page from nothing, word and image together. You will build yours the other way, uncovering a poem someone else already left on the page. You started the year learning to name the elements. You end it by inventing with all of them at once, on a page that is now yours.

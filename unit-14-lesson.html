Chuck Close and the Grid Method
Lesson
📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
An artist breaks one giant face into thousands of tiny squares and draws them one at a time. How can dozens of small, separate cells add up to a single, accurate, unified whole?
This module slows down on proportion: getting the relative size of every part right. You will learn the grid method, a centuries-old trick for transferring and enlarging an image accurately, and you will see how many small marked cells build one unified picture.
👀 PART 1 · LOOK FIRST

Today's work is still under copyright, so you will open it in another window to see it. Open the museum link below in a new tab, spend a full minute with the work, then come back. Let your eye travel it before you read another word.

Chuck Close worked at an enormous scale, but every enormous painting started small. In 1968 he took a single photograph of his own face, then ruled a fine grid over it in ink and marked it up with pencil and tape, exactly the document you can see at the museum link. That gridded photograph became his map. Working from it, he drew the same number of squares much larger on a canvas nine feet tall, then copied only what was inside one tiny square at a time: a smudge of gray here, a sharp edge there. He did not try to draw "a face." He drew one small box, then the next, then the next. Stand back from the finished nine-foot painting and the boxes disappear, and a face stares out at you, every feature in correct proportion.

Looking at the work in the museum tab: can you find the hand-ruled grid lines drawn right onto the photograph, along with the pencil notes and tape? Every one of those small squares eventually became a much larger square on a nine-foot canvas. How can someone get a nose, an eye, and a mouth all the right size relative to each other by only ever looking at one square at a time? Hold your answer. By the end of this lesson you will have the words for it.

📚 PART 2 · NAME WHAT YOU SEE

Proportion is the relationship in size between the parts of a work, and between each part and the whole. A portrait reads as a real face, or an animal as a real animal, only when the features are the right size relative to one another. Get the proportion wrong and the most careful shading in the world will still look off. Scale is closely related: it is the size of something compared to a standard, often the human body. Close's paintings are huge in scale, but their internal proportions stay true.

The grid method

The grid method is a way to transfer or enlarge an image accurately. You draw a grid of equal squares over a reference image. Then you draw a second grid, with the same number of squares, on your larger surface. Each square in the big grid matches one square in the small grid. You copy what you see inside one cell at a time. Because each cell is small and simple, your eye is not overwhelmed, and the relative positions stay locked in. Artists have used this method for centuries to scale up drawings accurately.

This is a public-domain example of the grid method from centuries before Close. It is not Close's own artwork or photograph, which you can view at the museum link above.

One cell at a time

The secret of the method is that you stop seeing the whole subject and start seeing one small box. Inside a single cell there are no eyes or noses, just light areas, dark areas, and a few edges where they meet. Picture one cell on its own: no face, no features, no fur pattern, only light and dark areas and the edge between them. That is far easier to copy accurately than a whole face or a whole animal. You match that one box, then move to the next.

When you forget you are drawing a face, an animal, or any recognizable subject, and simply match the values and edges in each square, accuracy takes care of itself. The proportion is already built into the grid.

From many cells to one whole: unity

Unity is the sense that all the parts of a work belong together as one thing. The grid method is a striking lesson in unity: you make dozens of separate decisions in dozens of separate cells, yet the consistent value range and the shared grid pull them into a single image. Step back and the lines and squares disappear. The whole reads as more than the sum of its boxes.

💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
If your finished drawing looks patchy, the usual cause is uneven value from cell to cell. Keep one value scale beside you and match each cell to it. Consistent values across all the cells are what create unity and make the grid lines melt away.
🔍 PART 3 · WATCH IT WORK

Artists and critics look at art in a deliberate order, so that judgment is earned and not just blurted. The four steps spell DAIJ: Describe, Analyze, Interpret, Judge. We will run them on Close's Study for Self-Portrait, the actual gridded photograph he used to build his monumental painting. Keep the museum tab open so you can look as you read. You will use the same steps in your VR/Critique this week.

Describe (just the facts)

A black-and-white photograph of a man's face, seen close up and head-on: glasses, tousled hair, a face filling most of the frame. Ruled directly over the photograph is a fine grid of ink lines, with pencil notes and a few small pieces of tape marking reference points.

Analyze (how is it built?)

Every square ruled onto this photograph corresponds to one much larger square Close drew on a nine-foot canvas, so the grid here is a proportion-transfer system, not decoration. Because the number of squares stays the same while the size of each square grows, the relative position and size of every feature, an eye, a nostril, a strand of hair, stays locked in no matter how large the final scale becomes. This document is proof of a working method: the strong unity of the finished painting, a single smooth face built from hundreds of separately rendered cells, starts here, in a grid ruled by hand onto an ordinary photograph.

Interpret (what is it saying?)

Close treats his own face the way a surveyor treats land: as a flat field of information to be measured and divided before it is ever enlarged. Seeing the grid itself, rather than only the finished painting, makes the point sharper. A faithful, monumental image is not a stroke of talent so much as a system, applied patiently, one square at a time.

Judge (does it succeed?)

It succeeds, and arguably it succeeds because it looks so unglamorous. A hand-ruled grid, taped and penciled onto an ordinary photograph, does not look like art on its own, yet it is the reason the nine-foot painting it produced holds together in accurate proportion. That is the power of proportion and the grid you will put to work in your own drawing.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND ENLARGING IMAGES
An AI tool can upscale or "enlarge" a photo to a larger size in one click, and it can add detail that was never in the original. It does not, however, build your eye for proportion the way copying an image cell by cell does. The point of this project is the seeing and the hand, training yourself to judge relative size and value and to transfer them accurately. That practice, and the drawing itself, are the work being graded here.
📝 VOCABULARY
ProportionThe relationship in size between the parts of a work and the whole.
ScaleThe size of a thing compared to a standard, often the human body.
Grid MethodCopying or enlarging an image by matching equal squares cell for cell.
CellA single square of a grid, copied one at a time.
UnityThe sense that all the parts of a work belong together as one whole.
LineA path made by a moving point; the grid is built from lines.
ShapeA flat, enclosed area; each cell holds small shapes of light and dark.
ValueThe lightness or darkness of a surface; consistent value builds unity.
EnlargementMaking an image larger while keeping its proportions accurate.
Reference ImageThe photo or picture you grid over and copy from.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

Proportion is what makes a picture believable, and the grid method is how artists keep it accurate while changing scale. Draw one honest cell at a time, hold your values steady, and dozens of small squares will fuse into one unified image. This week you will enlarge a picture you choose and watch the grid disappear.
